# **ANC screening strategies for bacterial STIs: a simulation study**

## **Overview**

This proposal outlines a simulation study to analyze screening strategies for asymptomatic bacterial sexually transmitted and reproductive tract infections during pregnancy. Using the STIsim model, we will evaluate different screening approaches to guide program design and implementation in Zimbabwe and potentially other similar settings. The study will consider gonorrhea (NG), chlamydia (CT), trichomoniasis (TV), and potentially bacterial vaginosis (BV).

## **Background**

Observational studies have documented associations between bacterial STIs during pregnancy and adverse outcomes. Maternal infections with gonorrhea, syphilis, or chlamydia are associated with increased risk of preterm birth, while bacterial vaginosis during pregnancy is a well-established risk factor for preterm birth and other complications. Chlamydia trachomatis infection is associated with preterm birth, low birthweight, premature rupture of membranes, stillbirth, and miscarriage, and Trichomonas vaginalis infection is linked to preterm delivery and low birth weight.

However, evidence from intervention trials has been mixed and sometimes disappointing. In particular, the WANTAIM trial in Papua New Guinea found that point-of-care testing and same-day treatment of chlamydia, gonorrhea, trichomoniasis, and bacterial vaginosis did not reduce rates of preterm birth, low birthweight, or both combined compared with standard care, despite successfully reducing STI prevalence.

The ambiguous findings of WAITAIM have generated some uncertainty about optimal screening approaches. Screening guidance for chlamydia in pregnancy varies among countries, potentially reflecting conflicting evidence regarding relationships with adverse outcomes and uncertainty about timing of infection. Screening guidelines vary widely across settings, and implementation is often limited by resource constraints.

## **Study Context and Relationship to PROMISE Trial**

This simulation study is designed to exist within a productive feedback loop with ongoing empirical research, particularly the PROMISE trial currently being conducted in Zimbabwe. The PROMISE trial (Protecting Mothers and their Infants through Screening and Comprehensive Management of Sexually Transmitted Infections in Antenatal Care) is an individually randomized controlled trial that will recruit 12,780 women across 15 antenatal clinics in Harare from January 2025 through December 2028\. The trial will test women for CT, NG, and TV at two time points during pregnancy (enrollment at ≤24 weeks gestation and third trimester at 32-34 weeks), with management based on test results including partner notification and treatment.

Our modeling work will complement and inform the PROMISE trial and similar empirical studies. 

Potential modeling / empirical trial feedback portals include:

* **Predictive modeling**: simulation studies can generate predictions about expected trial outcomes under different assumptions about transmission dynamics, timing of infection, reinfection rates, and partner treatment effectiveness. These predictions can be compared with emerging trial results to validate model assumptions and refine our understanding of causal pathways.  
* **Design optimization for future trials**: simulations can explore screening scenarios that extend beyond existing empirical trial design, including questions about optimal timing beyond the two-visit approach, whether screening could be effectively targeted to higher-risk populations rather than universal, whether all three pathogens need to be screened or if some provide greater value, and how different service bundling approaches (prophylactics, counseling intensity, partner notification strategies) affect impact. These findings can inform design of subsequent trials, implementation studies, or program rollout.  
* **Mechanistic insights**: By explicitly modeling transmission dynamics and pregnancy progression, we can identify which mechanisms are most likely to explain observed trial outcomes, whether positive or null. This can help explain why findings might differ between trials, and what contextual or design factors drive different outcomes across trials.  
* **Geographic extension**: simulation modeling can explore how findings might extend to other regions within Zimbabwe or to neighboring countries, helping identify which results are likely to be generalizable versus context-specific based on differences in STI prevalence, healthcare access patterns, and population demographics.

Interim simulation results should be presented at appropriate scientific convenings where PROMISE investigators and other trial teams are present, creating opportunities for iterative feedback, including: model parameter validation, Interpretation of findings, refinement of research questions,  and identification of key knowledge gaps that modeling could address to maximize scientific value of the trial investment.

This simulation study aims to create a genuine research partnership between empirical and modeling approaches, where each informs and strengthens the other. 

## **Research Questions**

1. **Optimal timing** When during pregnancy should screening occur to maximize health benefits?  
2. **Targeted versus universal screening** Can screening be effectively targeted to higher-risk populations based on age or geographic location, or should it remain universal?  
3. **Pathogen priority** Which bacterial infections (NG, CT, TV, and potentially BV) should be prioritized for screening if resources limit testing for all pathogens? Noting that the WANTAIM trial found that NG screening was most effective.  
4. **Service bundling** How should screening be integrated with complementary services such as prophylactic provision, STI prevention counseling, and partner notification to maximize impact?

## **Methods**

**Study design:** Simulation modeling using STIsim

**Setting:** Primary focus on Zimbabwe, with consideration of other Southern/Eastern African countries to enhance generalizability

**Approach:** Model various screening scenarios that differ by timing, target population, pathogen selection (NG, CT, TV, and potentially BV), and bundled services. Compare scenarios on infections averted, adverse outcomes prevented, cost-effectiveness, and implementation feasibility.
